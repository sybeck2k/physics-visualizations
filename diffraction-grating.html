Wavelets & the pattern

A plane wave hits the plate. Every open point of every slit becomes a new circular source (Huygens) — that is all the picture draws. Far away, the wavelets arrive with a path difference that depends on the angle, and add or cancel accordingly.

Inside one slit of width a, wavelets from the top and bottom edges differ by a·sinθ. When that equals one wavelength every wavelet finds a partner half a slit away that cancels it — the first dark null, a·sinθ = λ. Between the nulls: one wide bright band.

Wavelets from neighbouring slits arrive with a path difference d·sinθ. Where it is a whole number of wavelengths they add: d·sinθ = mλ, a bright fringe of order m. Where it is half a wavelength the two little arrows point opposite ways and you get darkness.

Wavelets from neighbouring slits arrive with a path difference d·sinθ. Where that difference is a whole number of wavelengths they add: d·sinθ = mλ, a principal maximum of order m. Where the N little arrows close into a ring, they cancel and you get darkness.

The bright directions are the envelope of the drawn wavelets — nothing asserts them. Switch the dashed order rays off and they are still there.

What the detector reads

Nothing at all until the light gets there — the plane wave has to cross to the plate, and the wavelets then have to cross to you. Watch the countdown, then the pattern lights up.

Slide the detector and you trace the screen curve in the left plot: a single broad central hump, dark nulls either side, and much weaker side‑lobes beyond them — nothing else. Width of the hump is set by the slit width a: narrower slit ⇒ wider pattern.

Slide the detector and you trace the screen curve in the left plot: evenly spaced bright fringes with dark gaps between, all under a slowly sagging single‑slit envelope set by the slit width a. The fringes are broad — two slits cannot make them sharp.

Slide the detector and you trace the screen curve in the left plot: tall narrow spikes at the orders, near‑black between them, all of it under a slowly sagging single‑slit envelope set by the slit width a.

Slide the detector and the screen curve is a single central spot — the m = 0 beam straight ahead — and nothing at any other angle. Drag the spacing back above λ and the missing orders switch on at the edge of the plot.

Power is printed as a percentage of the centre. In a null the reading is 0.0 %, not a small peak — the wavelets really do cancel.

Narrower slit ⇒ wider pattern

This is diffraction in its purest form: squeeze the light through a smaller gap and it spreads more. The central band is bounded by sinθ = ±λ/a, so halving a doubles its width.

Make the slit narrower than the wavelength and there is no null at all — the wavelets from one edge can never fall half a wave behind the other, and the light fans out across the whole half‑plane.

Everything else in this collection — double slits, gratings — is this pattern multiplied by something.

Two slits ⇒ fringes

Fringes sit at d·sinθ = mλ, so their angular spacing is about λ/d: widen the gap and they crowd together; use shorter light and they do too.

With only two wavelets the fringes are broad — soft cosine humps, not lines. That is the lesson of the next tab — add slits and watch each fringe sharpen to a line.

The envelope of the fringes is the single‑slit curve from tab 1: the pattern is the product of “two sources” and “one slit of width a”.

More slits ⇒ sharper

Adding slits does not just brighten the lines, it narrows them. Each principal maximum has angular full width ≈ 0.886 λ/(N d cosθ) — double N, halve the width. Sweep the N slider and watch the right plot slide down its 1/N slope.

That is why a grating is a spectrometer: two wavelengths λ and λ+Δλ separate when R = λ/Δλ = mN. The sodium D doublet at 589.0 / 589.6 nm needs R ≈ 982, so N ≳ 1000 illuminated lines at m = 1 — a double slit can never do it, however bright.

Even here, more slits still sharpen the one beam that survives — the m = 0 spot straight ahead — but there is no m = 1 to disperse colours, so no spectrometer.

How many orders fit

An order only exists if |mλ/d| < 1 — you cannot bend light past 90°. So the pattern has about d/λ orders on each side: more spacing ⇒ more orders, closer together; a spacing near one wavelength throws a single pair of orders almost to the horizon.

A single slit has no spacing d, so it has no orders — only its own nulls at a·sinθ = mλ. Switch to tab 2 to see orders appear.

A CD has a track pitch of 1.6 µm, so red light at 650 nm leaves it at asin(0.65/1.6) ≈ 23.9° — that is the rainbow you see on the disc. A DVD's 0.74 µm pitch throws red past 61°.

When the orders vanish

An order only exists if |mλ/d| < 1 — you cannot bend light past 90°. Squeeze the spacing below one wavelength and every order but m = 0 is gone: the light goes straight through, and the structure survives only as evanescent near‑field. That is the whole idea behind wire‑grid polarisers and metasurfaces.

A CD’s 1.6 µm pitch is comfortably above red light, so it still diffracts. Shrink the pitch to 0.4 µm (this tab) and the rainbow would be gone.

Reading the units

Nanometres (nm) — wavelength. Visible light runs 380 nm (violet) to 780 nm (deep red). A human hair is about 70 000 nm thick, so ~100 wavelengths of red fit across one.

Micrometres (µm) — slit spacing and width. 1 µm = 1000 nm. A CD track pitch is 1.6 µm; a good ruled grating is 1200 lines/mm, i.e. d = 0.83 µm.

Degrees — diffraction angle. 1° is roughly a fingertip held at arm's length; the full Moon is half a degree. A grating line 0.3° wide is a fifth of a Moon.

Per cent — relative power. 100 % is the centre of the pattern; 10 % is a tenth as bright, still obvious to the eye; 0.1 % looks black beside it.

Model caveats: scalar Fraunhofer (far‑field) diffraction from an ideal monochromatic plane wave at normal incidence. No polarisation, no obliquity factor, no finite‑source or slit‑thickness effects; the stage is drawn at micrometre scale and the screen distance is compressed. Treat side‑lobe levels as estimates, ±.
