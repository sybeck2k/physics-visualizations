Wavefronts & the cone

Every instant the vehicle radiates a pressure pulse that grows as a sphere at the local sound speed a. Because the source keeps moving, successive pulses sit off‑centre — packed ahead (Doppler), stretched behind.

At M=1 the pulses stack up at the nose; above it their common tangent is the Mach cone, half‑angle μ = arcsin(1/M) — faster ⇒ narrower. The many small shocks a real airframe makes only merge into one N‑wave after kilometres of travel.

Here M keeps rising, so the cone narrows as you watch. Starting from M≈0.7 the pulses bunch at the nose as it nears M=1 — the “sound barrier” — and the cone then trails behind for as long as it stays supersonic.

Here M is held fixed, so the cone angle never changes and the boom sweeps a continuous strip along the ground — not a one‑off bang at Mach 1. Drag the Mach slider and the whole cone re‑shapes.

The cone is the same geometry as ordinary cruise at this Mach — shaping the airframe does not change μ, only what the shocks are made of (see the next cards).

There is no steady cone here. The booster falls tail‑first and slows as the air thickens, so μ widens down the descent and the cone disappears altogether once the stage is no longer supersonic.

What the observer hears

Nothing until the cone sweeps past — then pressure jumps to +Δp, ramps through ambient to −Δp, and snaps back: the N‑wave. Its two shocks are a double boom once they're >~100 ms apart, a single crack when closer.

Nothing until the cone sweeps past — then, instead of a sharp N, a soft thump: a shaped airframe spreads its shocks so they don't pile into one jump. Watch the dashed “conventional” curve — the same airframe unshaped — for the crack it avoids.

A Falcon 9 booster returns tail‑first as a blunt, finned body: a base shock, a middle shock (grid‑fin compression meeting a landing‑leg expansion), and a shock off the top — a triple boom under ~1 s, arriving within seconds of touchdown.

An accelerating vehicle can also make a U‑wave: where successive cones converge the observer gets a focused, sharper‑peaked boom — the loudest spot, marked on the stage.

Off to the side, Δp fades to zero at the edge of the boom carpet (half‑width ≈ 2.6 × altitude). Beyond it: silence.

Off to the side, Δp fades quickly with distance from the pad, and beyond the boom carpet (half‑width ≈ 2.6 × altitude) there is silence.

Intensity & strength

Ground Δp grows with the vehicle's weight and cross‑section, weakly with Mach, and drops with altitude as √(p_source·p_ground).

A turn or hard acceleration focuses the cone into a U‑wave 2–5× stronger than steady flight — that is the peak the stage predicts before the run plays out. Concorde in cruise ≈ 2 psf for comparison.

Concorde in cruise made ≈ 2 psf (~100 Pa) under its track. Raise the altitude and Δp falls; stretch the vehicle length and it rises.

Loudness is really set by the equivalent‑area distribution — how gradually lift and volume build along the body. A long, slender, shaped nose (NASA's X‑59) keeps the shocks separate: a 0.3 psf, 75 PLdB thump vs Concorde's ~105 PLdB. Push past M≈1.4 and the shaping fades.

An F9 booster ≈ 2–7 psf near the pad. It arrives as a short, blunt triple boom close to touchdown. Entry Mach sets the strength aloft; what reaches the ground is capped by cut‑off.

Attenuation with distance

As it travels the N‑wave ages: cylindrical spreading (r^−½ — the cone is a line source, not a point) plus nonlinear steepening loss (r^−¼) give Δp ∝ r^−¾, while the duration grows as r^¼. Absorption also thickens the shock rise from ~1 ms to tens of ms — a crack becomes a rumble.

A shock only reaches the ground while its horizontal trace speed stays above the local sound speed all the way down. In level flight the cone is steady, so it does — the boom keeps arriving for as long as you fly. (Real Mach cut‑off for level flight sits near M≈1.15 in the stratosphere; only the booster tab models it.)

A shock only reaches the ground while its horizontal trace speed stays above the local sound speed all the way down. Once the source is slower than sea‑level sound it refracts upward — Mach cut‑off — which is why a decelerating booster falls quiet near landing. Wind and turbulence shift where this happens.

Reading the units

Δp — pascals (Pa) or lb/ft² (psf). The boom's pressure jump above still air (~101 000 Pa). 1 psf ≈ 48 Pa. ~50 Pa (1 psf) is a distant thud, ~100 Pa (2 psf) rattles windows, >~500 Pa (10 psf) can crack glass.

PLdB — perceived level, decibels. How loud and startling it seems, not the raw pressure. Logarithmic: +10 ≈ twice as loud. ~75 far rumble · ~95 loud boom · ~110 sharp crack.

T_N and rise — milliseconds. The N‑wave's length and how fast the shock jumps. A ~1 ms rise is a knife‑edge crack; tens of ms is a soft thud — same Δp, ~10–20 PLdB apart.
