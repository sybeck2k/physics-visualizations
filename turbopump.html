The trick: a tower of liquid

A pump is a spinning paddle that throws liquid outward. How fast its tip moves sets one thing: how tall a tower of liquid it can hold up. That height — the head, H ≈ ψ·u²/g — does not care what the liquid is.

Pressure is the weight of that tower over its base: Δp = ρ·g·H. Same height, so a heavy liquid presses hard and a light one barely presses. Oxygen is 16× denser than hydrogen ⇒ the same pump makes 16× less pressure with hydrogen.

The F-1 turns both its pumps on one shaft at 5,500 rpm. Oxygen and kerosene are only 1.4× apart in density, so one speed serves both — the kerosene pump just needs a bigger impeller.

To lift hydrogen's tiny weight to tens of megapascals the shuttle pump has to build a tower about 60 km tall. That is why it spins at 37,000 rpm and needs three stages.

Methane is 2.7× lighter than oxygen. At the same speed it gets 2.7× less pressure, so its pump must run faster or bigger than the oxygen one.

What one drop of liquid feels

The left plot follows a single drop through the pump. Pressure sags for an instant at the inducer, climbs through the spinning impeller, then climbs again in the diffuser as the liquid slows down.

Three stages ⇒ three steps. Each stage adds the same pressure, one after the other.

The dashed red line is the liquid's vapour pressure. Touch it and the liquid boils. Nothing shows on the plot until the drop gets there — the run starts with the drop at the inlet.

Why hydrogen needs stages

Head grows as tip speed squared, but a spinning disc can only go so fast before it tears itself apart — about 700 m/s at the rim here, twice the speed of sound in air.

When one stage cannot build the tower, you stack stages and add the heights. The readout Stages needed is that count at the speed ceiling.

The real Raptor stage count is not public. The model needs one stage at this ceiling, but treat that as an estimate.

The inlet is the weak spot

At the inducer the blade tips slice through liquid at high relative speed, and the local pressure dips below the tank's. If it falls to the vapour pressure the liquid boils into bubbles — cavitation — that choke the flow and hammer the blades.

The margin in metres of liquid is (p − p_v)/(ρ·g). A denser liquid weighs more per metre, so the same tank pressure buys fewer metres: raise ρ or the speed, or lower the tank pressure, and the red marker on the blade tip turns into bubbles.

Reading the units

Megapascals (MPa) — pressure. 0.1 MPa = 1 bar ≈ one atmosphere. 10 MPa is 100 atmospheres, the pressure about a kilometre under the sea.

rpm — shaft speed. A car engine on the motorway turns ~3,000 rpm; 5,500 rpm is 92 turns a second; 37,000 rpm is 617.

Megawatts (MW) — shaft power. A family car makes ~0.08 MW. The F-1's 41 MW is about five hundred of them driving one shaft.

Metres per second — tip speed. 140 m/s is a fast train; 700 m/s is twice the speed of sound in air.

Model caveats: a 1-D meanline pump — one velocity triangle per stage, a slip factor, assumed efficiencies, no leakage, no volute, no real 3-D flow. Pump discharge pressures are derived from reported power and flow; Raptor figures are estimates. The chips mark every source. Treat every figure as an engineering estimate, ±~30%.
