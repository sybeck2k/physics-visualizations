The trick

The probe falls into the planet's gravity well and climbs back out. It arrives at the well with speed w relative to the planet and it leaves with exactly that same speed — energy is conserved in the planet's frame. All the flyby does is turn w, by δ = 2·arcsin(1/e).

But the planet is moving, at 13.1 km/s. Your speed in the Sun's frame is v = v_planet + w. Turn w so it points more nearly along v_planet and that sum gets longer — you speed up ⇒ free Δv. Turn it the other way and the sum shortens ⇒ you brake.

This tab starts at b = +12 R_J: you pass behind Jupiter, w swings toward its motion, and the Sun‑frame speed rises by about +5 km/s.

This tab is the mirror image of tab 1: same speed, same depth, but b = −12 R_J — you pass in front. w swings the other way and the Sun‑frame speed drops by about 9 km/s.

Here the probe arrives going the other way round the Sun (α ≈ 172°), so w is huge — about 35 km/s. Even a modest turn (δ ≈ 49°) is worth roughly +9 km/s: the faster the arrival, the more a given turn buys.

The Voyager preset arrives at v∞ = 12 km/s from α = 130° and dives deep (δ ≈ 96°), for a Sun‑frame gain of roughly +13 km/s at these settings.

Nothing is free, of course: the probe pulls back on the planet just as hard, and Jupiter loses exactly the momentum the probe gains — about one part in 10²⁵ of its orbit.

What the probe measures

Watch the two speed curves. The green one is what an observer riding on the planet sees: it rises as the probe falls in, peaks at closest approach, and comes back down to the value it started at. Nothing happened. The encounter is elastic.

The amber one is the same motion in the Sun's frame — and it steps updown. Same probe, same instant, two frames, two different stories. The gain is real, and it is frame‑dependent: it is momentum borrowed from the planet's orbit.

Between the two, read the Frame check row: it prints how far apart the planet‑frame in and out speeds actually came out in the integration.

Where you aim

Two numbers set everything. b, the impact parameter, is how far off‑centre your incoming line misses the planet: small b ⇒ a deep pass ⇒ a big turn δ. And the sign of b picks the side.

Pass the trailing edge — the side the planet is retreating from — and the planet tows you forward: boost. Pass the leading edge and it pulls you backwards: brake. The right‑hand plot sweeps b across both sides at your current settings, so you can see the whole aim curve before you fly it.

You are on the boost side now. Drag the aim point toward zero and watch periapsis fall (≈3 R_J at the start) — a deeper pass turns you further, until the planet gets in the way.

You are on the brake side now (periapsis ≈3 R_J). Drag the aim point across zero to the other side and the Δv on the plot flips from a loss to a gain.

Here b = +8 R_J gives a shallower pass (periapsis ≈5 R_J) at a high approach speed. Fast arrivals bend less for the same aim, so you must aim deeper to turn as much.

Voyager's preset aims at b = +11 R_J (periapsis ≈4 R_J), on the trailing side. The real periapsis was far higher, because the real approach was out of plane — see the caveat below.

Aim too deep and you hit the planet — the red band on that plot. That, not physics, is the real limit on how much a single flyby can buy you.

Real missions

Voyager 2 at Jupiter, July 1979 was a trailing‑edge boost: its speed relative to the Sun rose from about 10 km/s to about 25 km/s (NASA/JPL trajectory data). Cassini strung together Venus–Venus–Earth–Jupiter flybys to reach Saturn — each one a boost like this tab, paid for in time rather than propellant.

Real missions

Parker Solar Probe runs the trick backwards: seven leading‑edge Venus flybys, each one shedding speed, to drop its perihelion inside 10 solar radii. Braking is how you fall toward the Sun — aiming at a planet that is moving toward you.

The same trick also bends a probe out of the ecliptic when you want a polar orbit.

Real missions

Anything launched from Earth is already circling the Sun the same way as the planets, so it arrives prograde. A retrograde arrival is a what‑if: no real probe flew this geometry at Jupiter.

It is useful because it is the extreme case — the largest relative speed w you can have, so the same turn δ changes the Sun‑frame speed by the most.

Real missions

Voyager 2 at Jupiter, July 1979. Its speed relative to the Sun rose from about 10 km/s to about 25 km/s across the encounter (NASA/JPL trajectory data) — a ~15 km/s gain no rocket it could carry would have given it. That single pass put it on a solar‑escape orbit; Saturn, Uranus and Neptune followed.

Voyager caveat: the preset reproduces the published speed change with a deeper ~4 R_J pass than the real ~10 R_J, because the true approach was out of plane.

Reading the units

km/s — speed. Earth circles the Sun at 30 km/s, Jupiter at 13.1. A chemical upper stage buys a deep‑space probe maybe 3–4 km/s of propellant budget in its whole life. So +10 km/s from one flyby is worth roughly a decade of rocket you could never launch — which is why every outer‑planet mission flies one.

MJ/kg — specific orbital energy. ε = v²/2 − GM☉/r, energy per kilogram of spacecraft. Negative ⇒ bound to the Sun. Zero ⇒ exactly escaping. Positive ⇒ gone. Jupiter moved Voyager 2 from about −28 to +3 MJ/kg.

R_J — Jupiter radii, 71 492 km. How close you skim. Aim at the trailing edge — the side the planet is moving away from, so you are pushed from behind — and you gain; aim ahead of it and you pay.

Model caveats: the planet rides a perfect circular, coplanar orbit and the probe is massless, so this is the circular restricted three‑body problem, not an ephemeris. The inset is a schematic. Treat every figure as an engineering estimate, ±~10%.
