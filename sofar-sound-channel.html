Rays bend toward slow water

Sound in the sea travels at about 1500 m/s, but not exactly: it speeds up in warm water and under pressure. A ray crossing that layered water refracts, and Snell's law keeps one number fixed all the way along it — cos θ(z) / c(z) = const.

So a ray always curves toward the slower water. Where the speed rises with depth the ray bends back up; where it falls with depth the ray bends down. In a layer of constant gradient g = dc/dz the path is exactly a circular arc of radius R = c₀ / (g·cos θ₀).

Wherever c(z) = c_source / cos θ₀ the ray goes momentarily horizontal — its turning point — and heads back. Steeper launch ⇒ deeper turn.

This ocean is the canonical mid‑latitude profile: c falls to a minimum on the axis, then rises again below it. Rays bend down above the axis and up below it — both sides push them back toward the minimum.

This ocean has a wind‑mixed layer on top: inside it, pressure alone makes c rise with depth, so rays launched in the layer bend up toward the surface instead of down.

This ocean is the deep channel plus a second, shallower notch in sound speed (near 280 m), so the profile has two minima and rays can be bent back toward either one.

This ocean has no minimum at all: under the ice, c rises steadily with depth (1435 + 0.0165·z m/s), so every ray bends upward.

The stage is drawn with a large vertical exaggeration (printed in the corner), because a ducted ray leans over less than 12° and would otherwise be a flat line. The small inset is the same fan at a true 1:1 aspect. Angles on the main stage are steepened; angles in the inset are real.

A speed minimum is a waveguide

Warm surface water is fast; cold deep water is slow; below about a kilometre the pressure takes over and speeds sound up again. That leaves a minimum — the SOFAR axis, near 1000–1300 m at mid latitudes.

A ray launched near the axis shallow enough (here within roughly ±13°) turns back before it can reach the surface or the seabed. It never touches either, so it never loses energy to scattering or bottom absorption — it just oscillates about the axis forever, spreading cylindrically instead of spherically.

With two minima there are two ducts. A source in the shallow notch traps its steepest rays there; flatter rays are carried on into the deep channel. The same click rides both.

That is why the 1991 Heard Island Feasibility Test put a source on the axis in the southern Indian Ocean and was detected on hydrophones roughly 18 000 km away, on both coasts of North America.

A mixed layer is a leaky waveguide

Wind churns the top of the sea into a uniform‑temperature layer. Inside it c rises with depth, so rays bend up, hit the surface, bounce, and bend up again — hop after hop, trapped between the surface and the layer's base.

Unlike the deep channel, this duct touches the surface. Each bounce loses a little energy, and a rough sea scatters far more than this calm‑water model does. Thicken the layer with the slider and more rays — steeper ones — are trapped.

Submarines sit just below such a layer on purpose: sound from a ship above can't easily get down to them.

Half a channel, with ice for a lid

The Arctic is cold from the top, so c has its minimum at the surface and rises with depth. There is only the lower half of a SOFAR channel: rays bend up, hit the underside of the ice, and bounce back down along a chain of arcs.

Because that duct is pinned to the surface, sound never stops touching it. Ice is rough and absorbs, so real Arctic propagation loses far more per bounce than the open‑water value used here — the model is flattering.

What the hydrophone hears

Not one arrival — a train. Each trapped ray takes a different path, so the same click reaches the phone several times. Steep rays swing far off the axis through faster water and arrive first; the near-axial ray takes the shortest, slowest path and arrives last and loudest.

So a SOFAR arrival is a rising stutter that ends in a bang — a crescendo cut off sharply. That signature is the fingerprint of the duct; drag the hydrophone and it reshapes.

What the hydrophone hears

Still a train, but a different one: each arrival is a ray with its own launch angle, and each has bounced off the surface a different number of times. Every bounce costs a little level, so arrivals are not simply “quiet early, loud late” as in the deep channel.

Drag the hydrophone below the duct and the train thins out and then stops — the surface‑trapped sound never gets that deep.

What the hydrophone hears

Here: nothing. Same ocean as tab 1, same source on the axis, but the hydrophone is up at 300 m — above the shallowest depth the fan ever turns. The arrival panel reads “no ray path”, and the transmission‑loss curve has nothing to mark.

Shadow zones sit beside loud paths

Rays leave gaps. Move the hydrophone a few hundred metres and you can fall in a shadow zone: geometrically no ray reaches you at all, while a phone on the axis at the same range is hearing a dozen arrivals. Loud and silent are neighbours.

The opposite also happens. Where neighbouring rays pile back together the tube collapses and the sound is refocused — a convergence zone, a ring of loud water every 50–65 km in the deep ocean, with quiet in between.

In a surface duct the shadow is beneath it: sound trapped near the surface leaks little downward, so a phone under the duct is quiet while a phone inside it is loud.

Turn on the turning-point envelope to see the caustics that bound both.

Reading the units

c — metres per second. The sound speed, ~1450–1550 m/s: a 1% change bends a ray by degrees. A whole ocean's profile spans about 100 m/s, less than the width of the c(z) panel.

TL — transmission loss, decibels. How much fainter than 1 m from the source. Logarithmic: +20 dB ≈ ten times fainter in pressure, and 70 dB of loss is a ten-million-fold drop in intensity. Ducted sound loses about 10 log R; unducted sound loses 20 log R — that gap is the whole trick.

Range — kilometres; arrival spread — seconds. 300 km is London to Paris; a blue whale call rides the channel across an ocean basin. The arrivals dribble in over a second or two — the drawn-out, stuttering echo of a shout in a canyon, not a single click.

This is a ray (high-frequency) model: no diffraction, no modes, no frequency dependence, flat seabed, no currents or eddies. Amplitudes are ray-tube estimates — treat every level as ±~5 dB and every count of arrivals as the strongest few.
